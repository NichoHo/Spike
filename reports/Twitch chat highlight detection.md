# Chat spikes flag moments, emotes name them

A Twitch chat spike is not the same as "funny". A spike means the audience reacted to something, and that something can be a joke, a clutch play, a jump scare, a sad moment, a raid, a sub train, or a stream-start "hi" flood. Message rate is still the strongest cheap signal on personality streams. On 2 million Twitch community clips, a model on chat frequency alone reached **AUC 0.875**, close to the best multimodal model at about 0.89 to 0.90 ([Song et al. 2021](https://epjdatascience.springeropen.com/articles/10.1140/epjds/s13688-021-00295-6)). But rate cannot say *what kind* of moment it was. The type comes from chat content: laugh emotes (LUL, KEKW, OMEGALUL, ICANT, "LMAO") versus hype emotes (Pog, W) versus confusion ("?????"). The same study found funny emotes grow more common as clips get more popular, while exciting emotes grow rarer. So an AI system should treat chat as a two-stage signal. First, a spike gate counts unique chatters after removing bots, commands, and event spam. Then a type label comes from which emote family dominates. That label should be fused with Spike's audio laughter and community-clip signals, and the LLM judges and trims the result. Spike's section 6 already has the right shape. It needs six concrete fixes. Split `chat_hype` into laugh and hype shares. Count unique chatters, not messages. Add a chat-cleaning step. Raise the 8 s chat lag default and estimate it per VOD. Count distinct clippers in `clip_heat`, not only views. Correct a misread benchmark that currently justifies the 0.5 LLM weight.

## A spike means "something happened", not "something was funny"

The academic record agrees that chat volume finds highlights of every kind. Song, Park, and Cha studied 213 Twitch streamers and used 2 million user-created clips as labels. They clustered emote embeddings into funny, exciting, surprising, nervous, sad, and general groups. The "epic moments" their model found spanned victories, fails, awkward and embarrassing moments, and even freestyle rapping ([Song et al. 2021](https://epjdatascience.springeropen.com/articles/10.1140/epjds/s13688-021-00295-6)). The most useful result for Spike is in their user study. **Clips chosen by human experts contained funny emotes 73% of the time, against 53% for clips the model chose**, and the model leaned toward surprising moments. A rate-heavy detector therefore drifts toward shock and hype, while humans pick for comedy. The authors also reported that the share of clips with funny emotes rises with view count, while exciting, nervous, and sad emotes fall. Danmaku research (scrolling comments on Bilibili) reached the same conclusion from another direction. Ping and Chen found that raw comment-spike selection fails because "comments at the beginning and end of a video are usually high-volume greetings and goodbyes". Adding emotion concentration and lag calibration lifted F1 from about 0.23 for spike selection to about 0.31 on 11 test videos ([Ping & Chen 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.02210)). That test set is tiny, and the mapping from table columns to methods is partly a reading of a garbled table. The direction is consistent with Song, though.

Rate is still strong, and an honest design keeps it as the backbone. In the only public evaluation against human Twitch clips, the median human-clipped moment sat at the **91.6th percentile of its VOD's chat rate**. 78% of moments fell in the top quarter, but **10.4% fell below the median, where no chat detector can reach them** ([cliptron README](https://github.com/infectedresearch/cliptron/blob/HEAD/README.md)). Ringer's thesis on League of Legends esports explains why content models sometimes fail to beat rate. In esports chat, a few tokens get spammed fast (a "cheering effect"), so content and density collapse into one signal. A message-density baseline (F1 0.190) was statistically tied with a content model that saw only one second of chat. A timestamp-aware transformer over 23 s of messages roughly doubled F1 to 0.416 ([Ringer 2022, §5.3.4](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/32406/1/PhD_Thesis__Revised_.pdf)). Two lessons follow. Content beats rate only when the model sees enough context and timing. On personality streams, content adds a few AUC points, not a step change: Song's deep chat-content model scored AUC 0.835 against 0.875 for deep chat frequency. For Spike, the payoff from content is not a better spike detector. It is the *type label* that the spike detector cannot give.

## Laugh emotes, unique chatters, and short repetitive messages separate the funny spikes

Emote families are distinct and easy to label, and Spike's current hype list mixes them. A peer-reviewed emote table defines PogChamp as "Amazement, for example, if the streamer shows extraordinary skill" and LUL as "General laughter or amusement" ([Kobs et al. 2020](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3365523)). An open-source highlight tool already ships the split as configuration. It defines Funny as `LUL, LULW, OMEGALUL, KEKW, LMF?AO+, LO+L`, and keeps separate Pog, Scary (monkaS), Sad (Sadge, PepeHands, BibleThump), Weird ("?" floods, WTF, bruh), and Surprise (D:) lists. Its README example is literally "top 10 funniest moments by counting the occurrence of the LUL emote in a 20-second window" ([joaohenggeler config](https://github.com/joaohenggeler/twitch-chat-highlights/blob/HEAD/Source/config.json.template)). Practitioner guides describe the same split. A "LUL spike" is a wall of LUL, KEKW, OMEGALUL, ICANT, and "LOL" and signals "a fail, a roast, or a clean joke". A "POG spike" is POG, "NO WAY", and caps "HOW" ([cliphunter.gg](https://cliphunter.gg/blog/twitch-chat-spike-detection)). Spike's `chat_hype` list puts `LUL`, `KEKW`, `OMEGALUL`, `LMAO`, `Pog`, `W`, `?????`, and `Clip it` into one share. That throws away exactly the distinction that Song's user study says matters.

| Family | Tokens (starting list, tune per channel) | What it means for Spike |
|---|---|---|
| Laugh | LUL, LULW, KEKW, KEKL, OMEGALUL, ICANT, pepeLaugh, LMAO, LMFAO, LOL, xd, HAHA+, 💀, 😂, 🤣; other languages: www, kkkk, jaja, ㅋㅋㅋ, 555, wkwk | The funny label |
| Hype | Pog, PogChamp, POGGERS, PogU, W, EZ, Clap, NO WAY, caps HOW | Clutch or skill; keep, but it is not "funny" |
| Confusion | "?" only messages, WTF, WutFace, D:, monkaS | Shock or cringe; with laugh tokens it often marks a fail |
| Sad | Sadge, PepeHands, BibleThump, F, o7 | Usually not a clip for a comedy source |
| Type-agnostic | "Clip it", "CLIP" | Strong "save this" intent, no type |

The table's token lists come from the sources above and from practitioner glossaries. They are a starting point, not a validated lexicon. No dataset labels emotes by emotion type. Kobs et al. labelled polarity only, and their lexicon is non-commercial ([emote-controlled](https://github.com/konstantinkobs/emote-controlled)). The multilingual laugh forms come from popular write-ups, not research ([Rest of World](https://restofworld.org/2023/how-people-laugh-online/)). Third-party emotes are not optional. In a 313-million-message study, **BTTV and FFZ emotes made up 44 of the top 100 most used emotes**, and emotes were a third of all unique tokens ([FeelsGoodMan, Dolin et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.08411v2)). Emote names appear as plain text in the message body, so Spike can match whitespace-split, case-sensitive tokens against the channel's BTTV, FFZ, and 7TV name sets without any image data. TwitchDownloaderCLI's `--embed-images` writes those names into the JSON as a side effect. Those are the channel's emotes at download time, not at stream time ([TwitchDownloaderCLI README](https://github.com/lay295/TwitchDownloader/blob/master/TwitchDownloaderCLI/README.md)).

Three structural features beyond emotes have support. First, **messages get shorter and more similar at highlights**. LIGHTOR built its detector on message count, message length, and message similarity for that reason ([LIGHTOR, ICDE 2020](https://www.cs.sfu.ca/~jnwang/papers/lightor.pdf)). Song found average utterance length negatively correlated with clip popularity, while emote frequency (rho 0.21) and sentiment strength (rho 0.25) were positive ([Song et al. 2021](https://epjdatascience.springeropen.com/articles/10.1140/epjds/s13688-021-00295-6)). Second, a cheap "agreement" feature (the share of messages equal to the single most common message) fires on emote walls and "?" floods even when total rate barely moves. That matters in small chats ([sqrtminustwo/twitch_auto_clipper](https://github.com/sqrtminustwo/twitch_auto_clipper/blob/HEAD/twitch_auto_clipper/TwitchAutoClipper.py)). These features measure how *strong* a reaction is, not whether it is funny. Third, unique chatters should replace raw message counts, so that one spammer cannot make a spike. Song normalized chat frequency and unique participants by follower count "to prevent the model from learning channel popularity". However, **no study compared unique chatters with raw message count head to head**, so this change rests on robustness reasoning, not a measured gain. Laughter shares should also count chatters, not messages, and should be shrunk toward the VOD mean (add-k smoothing). Otherwise two "LUL" messages out of two score 100%.

## Raids, sub trains, bots, and greetings make spikes that are not reactions

Every practitioner source names the same false positives: raids, sub trains, bot spam, giveaways, "lag complaints", and streamer-prompted chat. None publishes a filter. The cliptron author's first lesson from his own data was "Raids and emote spam outrank real clips" ([cliptron journal](https://github.com/infectedresearch/cliptron/blob/HEAD/journal.md)). Vendors advise suppressing "raids, sub trains, streamer-prompted chat moments" ([ClipMe](https://clipme.com/blog/chat-velocity-viral-moments)). The good news for a VOD tool is that most system events are missing from the chat replay. One developer's observation is that VOD replay omits subs, resubs, gift subs, raids, announcements, and channel-point redeems without a message ([twitch-archive #21](https://github.com/vEXOULZ/twitch-archive/issues/21)). The TwitchDownloader schema still has a `user_notice_params.msg_id` field and a per-message `bits_spent` ([ChatRoot.cs](https://github.com/lay295/TwitchDownloader/blob/master/TwitchDownloaderCore/TwitchObjects/ChatRoot.cs)). A 2022 regression set `msg_id` to null on every message, so the field cannot be trusted alone ([TwitchDownloader #393](https://github.com/lay295/TwitchDownloader/issues/393)). The bad news is that the *human* follow-on survives. When a raid lands or a gift-sub bomb fires, hundreds of real users post "W", "PogU", raid emotes, and thank-yous. That looks exactly like hype.

Cleaning therefore has to work on message text and user patterns. The rules below are inferred from the sources above, cheap, and untested at scale. Drop messages from a configurable bot list (Nightbot, StreamElements, Moobot, Fossabot, Streamlabs, Sery_Bot, and similar). Drop bodies starting with "!". Drop messages with a non-null `msg_id` or matching sub and gift patterns such as "subscribed at Tier" and "is gifting". Count each user at most once per bin. Detect raids as a burst of first-seen usernames that make up most of a window's chatters, and zero the chat signals for the next 60 s or so. Down-weight the first and last few minutes, where greeting and goodbye floods sit. Treat a window whose dominant token is a digit, "yes", or "no" as a streamer poll, not a reaction. Spike can confirm what the replay actually contains on a real VOD in Phase 0, with one `jq` group-by over `message.user_notice_params.msg_id`.

Chat size breaks naive normalization. A per-VOD z-score on sparse counts lets three messages look like an eruption. On huge chats, a few giant events dominate the spread. Practitioners say a stream at a 0.5 messages per second baseline "can't spike meaningfully" ([cliphunter.gg](https://cliphunter.gg/blog/twitch-chat-spike-detection)). This rule of thumb is unmeasured but plausible. Practitioners also recommend a rolling baseline, "median or exponentially weighted average over the last several minutes" ([ClipMe](https://clipme.com/blog/chat-velocity-viral-moments)). That handles a viewer count that climbs through a stream, which a single per-VOD median does not. Kleinberg's burst automaton is the textbook alternative, but it assumes Poisson arrivals that real message streams violate ([Zhao et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1411.3808)). Nobody has shown it beating simple baselines on Twitch. The lazy, defensible choice is to keep Spike's robust z-score on the log of the counts, against a rolling 10-minute median. Add a floor of a few unique chatters per window, and lower chat's fusion weight on small chats.

## Chat arrives 10 to 20 seconds late, and the delay varies by channel

Chat lags the event by stream latency plus reaction and typing time, and the measured lags are longer than Spike's 8 s default. Ringer swept the offset from minus 30 to plus 30 s on LoL Worlds broadcasts. **Shifting chat predictions 15 s earlier raised F1 from 0.416 to 0.529 (+27%)**, and positive shifts hurt, because "chat messages rarely indicate that an exciting moment is about to happen" ([Ringer 2022, §5.3.4.4](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/32406/1/PhD_Thesis__Revised_.pdf)). Ringer also notes that lag runs one way, so a symmetric smoothing kernel fits it poorly. LIGHTOR learned a constant of about 20 to 27 s between highlight start and chat peak on 2018-era Dota 2 and LoL streams ([LIGHTOR](https://www.cs.sfu.ca/~jnwang/papers/lightor.pdf)). Practitioners quote "10-20 seconds" ([cliphunter.gg](https://cliphunter.gg/blog/twitch-chat-spike-detection)), and a spike at 1:42:10 "usually means the moment started at 1:42:00 or earlier" ([ClipMe](https://clipme.com/blog/chat-velocity-viral-moments)). Cliptron reports chat peaks "35-44 s AFTER the true clip start" ([cliptron baselines.py](https://github.com/infectedresearch/cliptron/blob/HEAD/src/clipper/evals/baselines.py)). That number is measured from where a human clip *starts*, though, not from the event. A default Twitch clip publishes the last 30 s of a 90 s capture window, so it starts about 25 s before the click ([Twitch Create Clip](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/api/reference/#create-clip)). Subtracting that pre-roll leaves an event-to-peak lag of roughly 10 to 19 s, which agrees with the practitioner range.

The lag is a distribution, not a constant, and it changes per channel. Latency mode alone spans about 2 to 5 s (low latency) to 10 to 15 s or more (normal) ([Streamlabs](https://streamlabs.com/content-hub/post/how-to-reduce-stream-delay)). Some streamers add a deliberate delay against stream sniping. Replay sync bugs exist too. One TwitchDownloader user saw rendered chat "about 30-40 seconds in front of the clip" ([TwitchDownloader #1285](https://github.com/lay295/TwitchDownloader/issues/1285)). The esports figures carry 2018 to 2020 latencies, and low-latency mode is now common. So 8 s is probably short for most channels, and 15 to 20 s is probably long for low-latency ones. No source gives a 2024 to 2026 per-event lag distribution. The robust fix is to estimate lag per VOD. Cross-correlate the chat laugh share against the audio `laugh` series over lags of 0 to 30 s and take the argmax when the correlation peak is clear. Community clip ends can serve as a second anchor. Fall back to a default of about 12 s otherwise. Cliptron lists exactly this ("Learn the offset per channel") as its main future work. Two refinements are cheap. Anchor on the onset of the rise, not the peak, because big reactions keep building for several seconds. Danmaku work goes further and snaps each comment back to the first comment of its topical chain, on the observation that "the first comment about one shot usually occurs within the shot" ([Ping & Chen 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.02210)). That refinement is not worth the complexity for Spike yet.

## Community clips are the best second crowd signal and the best free label

Helix Get Clips works with an app token. It returns per clip `created_at`, `view_count`, `duration`, `creator_id`, `video_id`, `is_featured`, and, since July 2023, `vod_offset`: "the number of seconds from the start of the video to the beginning of the clip" ([Twitch API changelog](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/change-log/); [Helix reference](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/api/reference/#get-clips)). This settles the recalled fact in BLUEPRINT §6.1. Two caveats change the implementation. First, `vod_offset` can be null during the live broadcast and once the VOD is gone ([Clips guide](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/api/clips/)). Past broadcasts survive only 7 days for regular streamers, 14 for Affiliates, and 60 for Partners ([Twitch Help](https://help.twitch.tv/s/article/video-on-demand?language=en_US)). Spike should snapshot the clip list with offsets when it first ingests a VOD. Second, views and counts mean different things. Views follow a power law, where "only a few clips become popular" ([Song et al. 2021](https://epjdatascience.springeropen.com/articles/10.1140/epjds/s13688-021-00295-6)), and external sharing drives them. So `log(1 + views)` alone lets one viral clip swamp eight low-view clips of a different moment. The number of *distinct clippers* covering a second is the cleaner "the crowd agreed" signal. Views are better read as "this moment travelled". Because a default clip ends about 5 s after the click, the punchline tends to sit in the last part of a live clip, not the middle. Clippers also trim by hand, so the offset is only good to roughly plus or minus 5 s (inferred).

Clips also give Spike its evaluation set for free, and cliptron's protocol is worth copying. It merges human clips into "moments" with a 30 s gap, because "42 clips → 19 moments (~2x recall inflation if skipped)". It ignores clips under 5 views and reports Recall@10 ([cliptron metrics.py](https://github.com/infectedresearch/cliptron/blob/HEAD/src/clipper/evals/metrics.py)). Clips flagged `is_featured` are streamer-curated and make a stronger positive label. Clips still miss good moments that nobody clipped, so they are graded, noisy positives, not a complete answer key. Twitch's own AI Auto Clips, announced in October 2025 and based on signals like "positive excitement from the stream or funny banter", will show up as ordinary clips ([Twitch blog](https://blog.twitch.tv/en/2025/10/17/ten-years-of-twitchcon-here-s-what-we-announced-in-san-diego/)). That is useful, but it also means part of `clip_heat` may become Twitch's model rather than human viewers. No field marks such clips yet. Spike should check this when it first sees one.

Most other platform and external signals fail Spike's $0, app-token, VOD-only constraints. Stream markers are the most precise streamer-intent signal. Get Stream Markers is believed to need the broadcaster's or an editor's user token (recalled, not verified this session), so third-party channels are out of reach ([Helix reference](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/api/reference/#get-stream-markers)). No official API gives viewer count over time for a past VOD ([Twitch dev forum](https://discuss.dev.twitch.com/t/analytics-of-single-stream/45335)). Twitch removed Get Hype Train Events in February 2026 ([changelog](https://dev.twitch.tv/docs/change-log/)). The replay's `bits_spent` and gift-sub bursts are often scheduled or TTS-driven, so they work as weak features at best, not as funny signals. Among external crowds, a YouTube "Most Replayed" heatmap is valuable only when the streamer re-uploads the full VOD to YouTube. In that case it maps to the Twitch timeline with a constant offset found by audio cross-correlation, and Spike's existing `replay_heat` code applies. Reddit's r/LivestreamFail covers only the big moments of big streamers, and Reddit API access now needs approval ([SocialCrawl](https://www.socialcrawl.dev/blog/reddit-data-api-2026)). X's read API is paid. All three are optional extras, not core signals.

## Chat finds candidates, audio confirms, and the LLM judges and trims

Fusion beats any single modality, and simple late fusion captures most of the gain. On LoL Worlds 2020, Ringer's chat transformer (F1 0.534 after offset) beat audio alone (0.432) and visual alone (0.506). **Audio plus visual plus chat reached 0.642** ([Ringer 2022, §6.2.4](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/32406/1/PhD_Thesis__Revised_.pdf)). The robustness gain is larger than the average gain. The worst single game's F1 rose from 0.074 for chat alone to 0.410 for the fused model. Ringer traced this to the audio and chat models having the weakest output correlation: their noisy false positives do not line up. Chat also adds context that the other channels lack. The audience "is less likely to react strongly to a replay than to a live game event, even if visually, these segments are identical", and fusion suppressed such a replay. In other domains the chat contribution is smaller. It added only +0.8 to +2.5 F1 on top of video in Fu et al.'s esports study ([Fu et al. 2017](https://aclanthology.org/D17-1102.pdf)). Removing comments plus ASR cost 2.0% against 4.7% for removing visuals in Kuaishou's industrial model ([KuaiHL](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12002)). Fusion added about 1 AUC point on Song's personality-stream data. No study has fused chat with *laughter* on non-esports Twitch streams, so Spike's specific chat-plus-YAMNet combination is supported by analogy, not measured.

The LLM's place in this picture is narrow and clear. Feeding chat to an LLM as its only input "resulted in a significant drop in performance across all models" in a 2026 Twitch clip study, and newer Gemini models understood clips from audio-visual input alone ([Lindroos et al., GamiFIN 2026](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3789624.3789641)). Chat belongs in the prompt as context about how the crowd reacted, not as the thing to judge. The best practitioner summary is cliptron's: "chat finds candidates, model does boundaries and tie breaks" ([cliptron journal](https://github.com/infectedresearch/cliptron/blob/HEAD/journal.md)). Its agent, which snaps boundaries to Whisper segments, cut the median boundary error only from 30.8 s to 28.6 s. One correction matters for Spike. The earlier research report and BLUEPRINT §6.4 cite cliptron's "tuned chat-rate Recall@10 0.917 vs 0.708 for the LLM agent" as proof that chat beats an LLM. That comparison covers **4 VODs and 13 moments from one channel**, and the chat baseline was tuned per VOD on the same answer key it was scored against. The README calls the gap "2.7 moments". Across all 13 VODs, untuned chat rate scored **Recall@10 0.295** (tuned 0.360) against 0.060 for random ([cliptron README](https://github.com/infectedresearch/cliptron/blob/HEAD/README.md)). Chat is a strong candidate generator, roughly 5 times random. The data does not show that it out-ranks an LLM, so Spike's 0.5 LLM weight for chat VODs needs a different justification, or an eval.

## What to change in BLUEPRINT section 6

The changes keep section 6's shape and mostly replace things rather than add them. The new fusion set for VODs with chat is below. The weights are still starting guesses for §6.5 to re-fit. The main shift moves weight from raw rate and the mixed hype share into a laugh share. The without-chat set is unchanged.

```
with chat:    hype(t) = 0.20 * chat_users + 0.20 * chat_laugh + 0.20 * clip_heat
                      + 0.15 * laugh + 0.05 * chat_hype + 0.05 * chat_agree
                      + 0.05 * audio_loud + 0.05 * shout + 0.05 * speech_rate
```

Two things should not change. The robust per-VOD z-score stays, applied to log counts. Spike's late weighted-sum fusion stays too. Ringer's gains came from plain late fusion, and no source shows a learned fusion model helping at Spike's data scale.

## Conclusion

The research reframes chat from a score into a sentence with a verb and an adjective. The unique-chatter spike says *when* the crowd reacted. The emote family says *how* it reacted. The lag estimate says *what they were reacting to*, about 10 to 20 seconds earlier. Spike's current design blends all three into two numbers, and that hides the one distinction its users care about. The single most promising untested idea is to align chat to the streamer's own audio laughter per VOD. It solves the lag problem with no labels, and it doubles as a check that the laugh detector and chat agree. Nothing in the literature has tried it on personality streams.

The evidence base is thinner than its citations suggest. The rigorous chat studies are mostly LoL esports from 2017 to 2022, the one large personality-stream study predates 7TV, and the only Twitch funny-moment benchmark covers 13 VODs from one programming streamer. No public labeled dataset of funny Twitch moments exists. Spike's frozen eval set, built from merged community clips plus review labels, will be one of the few in the field. The weights in the table above are reasoned starting points that the first 10-VOD eval will overwrite. That is how they should be treated.
